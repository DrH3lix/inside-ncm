Inside a Next-Concept Model

The Next-Concept Model, from Stanford's Hazy Research and Scaling Intelligence labs, writes a story coarse to fine instead of word by word. It still has a J-space, the small set of word-shaped directions Anthropic found inside Claude, but in a different place: at its content levels, hidden states line up with word directions —× to —× more than a rotated null at their first and last layers, against —× at best in an ordinary word-by-word model trained on the same stories. Editing it shows a division of labor: the level you edit sets how much of the story changes, and the lens direction sets what it changes to.

How NCM writes a story

A word-by-word model writes left to right. NCM writes the whole story at once as a stack of — levels: first a single code, then more and more codes, each level adding detail to everything before it. Decoding the draft after each level shows the cast and setting arriving before the grammar, and the model changing its mind on the way.

Does it have a J-space?

A Jacobian lens asks, for every layer, how nudging a hidden state changes the model's eventual text, and turns each direction back into a word. We then ask how much of each hidden state a non-negative mix of — word directions explains, compared with the same hidden states randomly rotated. Rotation keeps their size and spread but destroys any alignment with words, so the ratio measures real alignment, not what a big dictionary can fit to anything.

What it means

  • Both models have word-aligned directions, so both have something J-space-like.
  • In NCM the alignment is strongest at levels three to six, the levels that carry the story's content, and at the first and last layers of each level.
  • Mid-stack the alignment dips while the effective rank peaks: the generator does its coordination in a wider space that doesn't look like words.
  • Level two is one direction. Its high ratio is real but carries no single word.

How much each level can move the story

The size of each level's Jacobian is its leverage over the final text, and its effective rank is how many independent directions it uses. Leverage falls by orders of magnitude from coarse to fine. In nearly every level the rank peaks in the middle layers; the replication table below has the count.

Swapping dog for cat

The causal test. We pushed hidden states along the lens direction for "cat" minus "dog" and let the rest of the story generate with the same random seed, against a random push of the same size. In NCM, the level you edit sets how much of the story changes, for any push: a random push of the same size changes about as much, but never adds a cat. The lens direction decides what the story changes to. In the word-by-word model, the edit only reaches the future.

Follow-up: Sudoku, and swapping the early levels

NCM also ships Sudoku checkpoints. The puzzle is the prompt, and the model writes the 81-digit solution coarse to fine over 12 levels. Sudoku has a right answer for every cell, so it's a sharper test of the menu and suppression results above. Claude wrote its predictions to disk before any data; for the swap test at the end of this section, Gemini made sealed predictions too. On — puzzles per difficulty with greedy decoding, the model solves — of hard puzzles and — of easy ones exactly.

Do the wrong candidates follow the rules?

Of the wrong digits still in play in a blank cell (p ≥ 0.1), the share the clues allow, meaning no clue in the same row, column or box rules it out. Dashed: chance.

The middle drafts don't look like pencil marks. From level 1 to level 11, the wrong digits still in play are ones the clues allow — to — of the time, close to the — you'd get by chance. The rules show up at the last level, which has one code per cell: when the model writes a wrong digit there, the clues allow it — of the time (— on easy puzzles). Our reading, which we haven't tested: in this model, coarse to fine is a blurry copy of the answer that sharpens, not solving by elimination.

By the measure used for the stories (change since the starting level), dropped digits fall well below digits that were never considered, from every starting level. In absolute terms they still finish 1 to 3 logits above them: the model pushes dropped digits down from the top but doesn't bury them below the field. The story test above measured change only, so we can't say whether the stories differ on this.

How firmly do the early levels commit?

The NCM authors hypothesize that "coarse-to-fine generation supports global planning by establishing an overall structure before resolving finer details." To measure how firmly that early structure commits the model, we gave each puzzle the first few levels of codes the model had generated for a different puzzle, then let it generate the remaining levels itself, still prompted with the right puzzle. It mostly recovers. With levels 1–6 swapped it still solves — of hard puzzles (against — with its own codes) and — of easy ones. The answer locks in at levels 7–11: swapping through level 9 leaves —, through level 11 none. Even then the model doesn't write the other puzzle's grid. The last level alone brings — of the blank cells back to the right answer, and only — take the other puzzle's digit. Another puzzle's codes hurt less than random codes (— against — with levels 1–6 swapped), so the early levels are being used. The plan they set is soft: later levels can override it.

Added after the authors replied

This is by design. As the authors pointed out to us, the model sees the puzzle at every level, and NCM is trained with conditioning corruption: during training, a fraction of the codes it conditions on are replaced with random codes from the same level's codebook while the targets stay correct, so later levels learn to recover from errors earlier in the hierarchy (paper, Section 3.4 and Appendix F.3). A hard plan would leave early mistakes uncorrectable. So this swap measures how soft the plan is; it isn't evidence against planning. The blog post mentions this training step too, and we missed it when designing the test.

The same swap on stories

Gemini proposed that what decides whether early codes stick is whether the prompt contradicts them. The TinyStories model has no prompt at all (every story starts from the same single token), so we ran the identical swap there: story A is what a random seed produces; the swap run uses the same seed, so the same random draws, but forces levels 1 through k to another story B's codes. With nothing to pull it back, the early codes become the story. Swapping level 1 alone sends it somewhere new: only — of A's distinctive words survive, and B's show up at chance. From level 3, — of stories follow B, though at that point only — of B's distinctive words have arrived; the rest is written level by level (— by level 6, — by level 11). Sudoku at a similar depth: 0.7%. Same model family, same training trick, opposite outcome. Model and task differ too, so prompt strength may not be the only cause.

Method and limits

  • Models. Next-Concept Modeling, from Stanford's Hazy Research and Scaling Intelligence labs. TinyStories checkpoint: a generator with — layers plus a VQ-VAE tokenizer. Control: TinyStories-33M, an ordinary GPT-Neo with — layers, trained on the same stories. Sudoku: NCM's sudoku-easy and sudoku-hard checkpoints (an 8-layer generator with the puzzle as its prompt), on the first 1,000 test puzzles of each.
  • The predictors. Claude Opus 5.5 (high reasoning effort), working inside the project with the code and setup in view; Gemini 4 Argon (low thinking level), given DJ's short description of each test. Same settings for every round. "Argon" in the lab notebook is Gemini 4 Argon. Different information and settings, so the scorecards are a record of the predictions, not a controlled comparison of the models.
  • The lens. After Anthropic's J-space work: the average effect of each layer's hidden state on the model's eventual text, summed over the whole story, mapped into words through each model's own output matrix. For NCM the path runs through the level's code head (straight-through, so the decoder still sees the real story) into the tokenizer's decoder. Each lens was fit on — stories.
  • Direct effects only. NCM's lens holds later levels fixed, so influence that travels through later levels' generation isn't counted. That indirect influence could add to the direct effect or partly cancel it. The swap test does let later levels respond, and there an edit at level five still rewrote the whole story.
  • Hardware and time. One consumer GPU with — GB. Fitting NCM's lens took about — minutes and the control's about —; decoding four stories level by level took — seconds. The weights are small: — MB for NCM and — MB for the control.
  • Small samples. Each swap condition used — stories, and each lens was fit on 64; the Sudoku tests used 1,000 puzzles per difficulty and the story swap 128 stories on each of two seeds. This is directional evidence from a few evenings' work, not a paper.
  • Different vocabularies. The two models read out through different word lists, so raw fits aren't comparable across models. The ratio to each model's own rotated null is.
  • Interpretations. Explanations for why level three kept the dog, or why early control layers produced "Mouse", are hypotheses, not measurements.
